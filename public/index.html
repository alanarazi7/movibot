🔎 Retrieval

Plot synopses are split into passages before being embedded, rather than embedded whole. The embedding model accepts 512 tokens; the median synopsis is 1,143 and the longest is 9,049, so one vector per film covered only the opening of each story.

Frozen is the case that exposed it. Hans first appears at character 5,353 of 27,969 and betrays Anna at character 22,746 — 81% of the way through a synopsis whose vector reached roughly the first 7%. No phrasing of “a film about trusting the wrong person” could retrieve it, and reading the film's text returned the opening scene rather than the beat that matters. It is now passage 109445_21:

…and says “Oh, Anna, if only there was someone here who loved you!” As Anna looks at him in shock, Hans explains that as the youngest of 13 brothers, he had no chance at claiming his family's throne, so he went looking for a royal family he could marry into…

Why sentences, not paragraphs

The obvious approach is to accumulate paragraphs up to a token budget. Measured against this corpus it does nothing: zero of the 159 synopses contain blank-line paragraphs and 66 have no newline at all, so a paragraph splitter would emit one chunk per document. Passages are assembled from sentences instead, at 300 tokens with 20% overlap — about 13 sentences, roughly one scene, tight enough that a story beat dominates its passage instead of being averaged into a dozen others. 1,254 passages from 159 films.

Every passage for every film is in the 🗃️ Data tab — open a row and expand its passages.

🧰 How this catalog was built

Three sources go in. A Kaggle catalog supplies the structure — ids, dates, runtime, genres, studios, votes, keywords — and is the only one that supports deterministic filtering, so it defines which films exist. Its descriptions are too thin to reason about, at a median of 53 words, so MPST plot synopses supply real story text at a median of 892 words, roughly 17× longer. Wikipedia supplies independent verification text, scraped once offline: the Plot section for scene questions, and everything else — cast, production, reception — for tone questions the plot cannot settle.

Why only Disney and Pixar

It keeps the catalog in family territory and makes the demo coherent, but the real reason is a constraint: the assignment caps stored data at 50 MB and the full multi-studio catalog does not fit. The same pipeline produces all 43,270 films with the studio filter turned off.

Why nothing after 2017

The Kaggle dataset is a snapshot published in 2017, so its metadata simply stops there. This is a property of the source, not a filter we chose: the newest films in the catalog are Cars 3, Beauty and the Beast, and Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 2, all 2017, and 21 films date from 2015 or later. Anything since — Frozen II, Encanto, Turning Red — does not exist as far as this system is concerned. The agent is told this explicitly and refuses questions about recent or current releases rather than answering them from a stale catalog.

Duration filter

Disney and Pixar both release short films alongside features, and a six-minute short is never what someone asking for “a movie” wants. Anything under 45 minutes is dropped, which removes 66 titles.

Ranking

Recommendations should surface the best and most widely-seen films, and rating alone cannot do that: a film scoring 8.0 from 140 votes would outrank one scoring 8.0 from 5,520. Each film therefore carries a weighted_rating — the formula IMDb uses for its Top 250 — which pulls thinly-voted films toward the catalog average until they earn enough votes to stand on their own: 

weighted_rating = (v / (v + m)) × R  +  (m / (v + m)) × C

R = the film's rating     v = its vote count
C = 6.199 catalog mean    m = 300 vote prior

It ranks rather than filters, so nothing is removed and no answer becomes unreachable — thinly-voted films are demoted on broad queries and still win narrow ones where they are the right answer. Sort the table by Best rated to see the result.
